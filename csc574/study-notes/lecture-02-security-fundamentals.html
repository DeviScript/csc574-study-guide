Security Fundamentals

The central idea

A system is secure only in context. We must specify the properties the system should preserve and the adversaries who may try to violate them.

Security modelA combination of a trust model, which describes what we rely on, and a threat model, which describes adversaries and their capabilities.

The CIA triad

Confidentiality prevents unauthorized disclosure. Integrity prevents unauthorized modification. Availability preserves authorized access to systems and information.

These properties can conflict. An ATM that stains stolen cash protects integrity, but sacrifices availability. Emergency responders may sacrifice confidentiality by falling back to an unencrypted radio channel when availability matters most.

Security vocabulary

Assets are what we protect: systems, access, people, attention, data, money, and reputation. Participants are entities in the system, including users, hosts, clients, servers, and agents.

An adversary attempts to circumvent security. Adversaries may be curious users, criminals, competitors, nation-states, environmental events, or even accidental mistakes, depending on the system.

A vulnerability is a flaw that exposes an asset to a threat. A threat is a possible danger that may exploit a vulnerability. Risk combines likelihood, vulnerability, and cost.

risk = threat likelihood × probability of successful exploitation × cost

Attacks and defenses

Attack archetypes include interception, modification, fabrication, and interruption. Real attacks often chain several types together, such as a phishing message followed by credential theft and unauthorized account changes.

Defense archetypes include prevention, deterrence, detection, recovery, and resistance. Backups support recovery; logging supports detection; shielding an ATM keypad can reduce interception.

Threat modeling

Threat modeling is iterative. Scope the system, map data flows and trust boundaries, identify threats, rank their risks, choose mitigations, and reassess the result. STRIDE is one useful framework: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, and elevation of privilege.

Trust and economics

Authentication asks who are you? Authorization asks what are you allowed to do? A trusted component is one the system depends on for security. A trustworthy component is one for which we have reason to expect correct behavior.

The principle of adequate protection says that assets should be protected to a degree consistent with their value. Security has costs in money, performance, resources, and complexity, so the strongest control is not automatically the best choice.
