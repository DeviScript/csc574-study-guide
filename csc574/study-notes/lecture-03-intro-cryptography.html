Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Define cryptology, cryptography, cryptanalysis, plaintext, ciphertext, encryption, decryption, key, keyspace, and perfect secrecy.
  • Define Kerckhoffs’s principle and explain why cryptosystems should conform to it.
  • Identify key management problems.
  • Explain types of cryptanalysis.
  • Explain the one-time pad (OTP), how it provides perfect secrecy, and the practical issues that complicate its use.

Answers

Define cryptology, cryptography, cryptanalysis, plaintext, ciphertext, encryption, decryption, key, keyspace, and perfect secrecy

  • Cryptology: the broader study of secure communication and code systems.
  • Cryptography: developing and using cryptosystems.
  • Cryptanalysis: studying how to analyze or break cryptographic systems.
  • Plaintext: the original readable message.
  • Ciphertext: the encrypted representation of the message.
  • Encryption: transforms plaintext into ciphertext.
  • Decryption: transforms ciphertext back into plaintext.
  • Key: an input used by a cryptographic algorithm to maintain security against an adversary.
  • Keyspace: the set of all possible key values.
  • Perfect secrecy: ciphertext does not change the adversary's knowledge of which plaintext was sent.

Define Kerckhoffs’s principle and explain why cryptosystems should conform to it

  • Security should rely on individual secret keys, not hidden algorithms.
  • A cryptosystem should remain secure even if everything about the system except the key is public knowledge.
  • Public designs can receive broader review and analysis.
  • If a key is compromised, replacing the key is more realistic than replacing a secret algorithm.

Identify key management problems

  • Lifecycle: generation, distribution, rotation, revocation, storage, backup, and destruction.
  • Recipients: who gets which keys.
  • Distribution and storage: how users securely receive and store keys.
  • Export: what happens when a key is exported.

Explain types of cryptanalysis

  • Attack settings: ciphertext-only, known-plaintext, chosen-plaintext, and chosen-ciphertext.
  • Common approaches: brute force, linear cryptanalysis, differential cryptanalysis, side channels, and coercion.

Explain the one-time pad (OTP), how it provides perfect secrecy, and the practical issues that complicate its use

A one-time pad encrypts a message by XORing it with a shared, secret, uniformly random key of the same length. It provides perfect secrecy only when the key is random, used once, kept secret, and destroyed after use.

  • One-time pad: an encryption method where Alice and Bob share a secret, uniformly random key $k$ that is the same length as the message $m$.
  • Encryption: $c = m \oplus k$.
  • Decryption: $m = c \oplus k = (m \oplus k) \oplus k$. This works because $(A \oplus B) \oplus B = A$.
  • Requirements: a uniformly random key, a key as long as the message, and no key reuse. After use, the key should be destroyed.
  • Perfect secrecy: every candidate plaintext remains possible under some key, even against an adversary with unlimited computation.
  • Practical issues: random key generation, secure key distribution, message-length keys, no key reuse, and no built-in integrity.
  • Key reuse: reusing the same key can expose a relationship between both plaintext messages and destroys the perfect secrecy property.

Cryptography foundations

In this course, crypto means cryptography, not cryptocurrency. Cryptography is the art and science of secret writing: a toolbox that can provide selected security properties, rather than security in the abstract.

Crypto toolbox

The lecture's toolbox begins with plaintext and ciphertext, keys and algorithms, and encryption and decryption. A cryptosystem is evaluated against its security properties, threat model, and attacker capabilities.

Core terminology

  • Cryptosystem: a method of disguising plaintext so only selected parties can recover it.
  • Cryptography: developing and using cryptosystems, including mechanisms such as encryption, hashes, MACs, and signatures.
  • Cryptanalysis: analyzing or breaking cryptographic systems.
  • Cryptology: cryptography plus cryptanalysis.
  • Plaintext or cleartext: the original readable message, before encryption or after successful decryption.
  • Ciphertext: the encrypted representation of a message.
  • Encryption: converts plaintext into ciphertext; decryption converts ciphertext back into plaintext.
  • Cipher: a cryptosystem's encryption and decryption procedures for turning plaintext into ciphertext and recovering the plaintext.

Participants and threat model

Alice and Bob are placeholder names for communicating parties. Alice commonly sends and Bob receives. Eve is a passive eavesdropper; Mallory is an active, malicious attacker; and Trent is sometimes a trusted third party. Other names, such as Carol or Charlie, represent additional participants.

  • Dave or Daphne: a fourth participant; Oscar: an opponent.
  • Peggy and Victor: the prover and verifier in a zero-knowledge proof.
  • Sybil: an attacker using multiple identities; Ivan: an insider. Names such as Erin and Frank represent further participants as needed.

The basic lecture threat model places Alice and Bob on an untrusted public channel where Eve/Mallory can observe messages. Eve's initial goal is to read the message; Mallory may also modify or forge messages. Cryptography protects the message even though the channel is not trusted.

What cryptography can provide

  • Confidentiality: keep data and communication secret by encrypting before sending or storing it and decrypting only for authorized parties.
  • Integrity: detect unauthorized changes or tampering. Integrity does not necessarily stop a change; it lets the recipient detect that the message was altered.
  • Authenticity: bind data to an expected origin and verify who created or sent it, such as checking whether Alice really sent a message.
Important distinction: encryption alone does not automatically provide integrity or authenticity.

Where encryption appears

  • Websites and networks: TLS, X25519, AES-GCM, and ChaCha20-Poly1305.
  • Wi-Fi: WPA2, WPA3, AES-CCMP, and SAE.
  • Messaging: Signal Protocol, X3DH, and Double Ratchet.
  • Storage: full-disk encryption with AES-XTS and platform key stores; password managers and backups with Argon2, PBKDF2, AES-GCM, or ChaCha20-Poly1305.
  • VPNs and files: WireGuard, IPsec, TLS, age, GPG, and AES.

Efficient cryptographic primitives earn trust through extensive public analysis and no known practical attacks, not through a simple proof that every attack is impossible.

How the three properties look on an untrusted channel

  • Confidentiality: without encryption, Eve can read Alice's "Hello!" on the public channel. With encryption, Eve sees ciphertext while Bob decrypts and recovers the message.
  • Integrity: without an integrity check, Mallory can change "Hello!" to "Hell!" and Bob cannot tell. With an integrity check, Bob detects the alteration.
  • Authenticity: without proof tied to Alice's expected identity or key, Mallory can forge a message that appears to be from Alice. Verification lets Bob reject a message that does not verify under Alice's expected key.

Cryptography is not complete security

Strong cryptography does not by itself solve buffer overflows, malware, SQL injection, cross-site scripting, unsafe logging, compromised endpoints, malicious administrators, access-control failures, or poor programming practices. Algorithm choice, parameters, implementation, key handling, APIs, and defaults all matter. Do not roll your own cryptography. Failures can be difficult to notice because a program can still appear to work.

Cryptosystems and keys

A cryptosystem turns readable information into protected information and recovers it correctly. Alice encrypts a plaintext message $M$ using encryption algorithm $E$ and key $K_e$ to produce ciphertext $C$. Bob uses decryption algorithm $D$ and key $K_d$ to recover the plaintext.

M → Encryption (E + Ke) → C → Decryption (D + Kd) → M

Formal cryptosystem and correctness Slide 26 Slide 27

A formal cryptosystem is the 6-tuple (E, D, G, M, C, K):

  • E: encryption algorithm.
  • D: decryption algorithm.
  • G: key-generation algorithm.
  • M: message space, the set of plaintexts.
  • C: ciphertext space, the set of encrypted outputs.
  • K: key space, the set of possible keys.
E(Ke, M) = C    D(Kd, C) = M    D(Kd, E(Ke, M)) = M

The final relation is correctness: decrypting a message with the appropriate key must return the original plaintext.

Symmetric and asymmetric cryptography Slide 28

Secret-key or symmetric Public-key or asymmetric
Ke = Kd; the same shared secret key encrypts and decrypts. It is generally fast, but secure key distribution is the main challenge. Ke ≠ Kd; different keys support public-key encryption, key agreement, and digital signatures. It is usually slower than symmetric cryptography.

Keys, key space, and security level

A key is an input to a cryptographic algorithm used to maintain security in the presence of an adversary. Keys are generally numbers; a password can help derive a key, but a human password is not automatically a strong cryptographic key. Some key values have special mathematical properties. A key space is the set of all possible key values.

  • For a well-designed symmetric cipher, a longer key generally raises the cost of brute force.
  • Algorithm weaknesses can reduce effective security even when keys are long; key lengths are not directly comparable across algorithm families.
  • About 80-bit symmetric security is a legacy level and should not be used for new systems; 128-bit security supports roughly a decade of protection, and 256-bit security supports protection over several decades.
  • Harvest now, decrypt later, also called steal now, decrypt later or retrospective decryption: adversaries can collect encrypted data today and decrypt it later as computing power, attacks, key compromise, or quantum technology improves. Long-lived secrets may need stronger protection now.

Kerckhoffs's principle and key management

A cryptosystem should remain secure even if everything about it except the key is public. Security should rely on a secret key, not a hidden algorithm or implementation. Public designs receive broader review, and replacing a compromised key is more practical than replacing a deployed secret algorithm.

  • Shannon (1949): assume the enemy knows the system being used.
  • Bellovin (2009): design the system assuming opponents know it in detail.
  • Obscurity can slow casual analysis, but it is a weak primary security argument: a short key is easier to protect and replace than an entire algorithm.

Key management asks who receives keys and how they are generated, distributed, stored, rotated, revoked, backed up, exported, and destroyed. Keys may be stored in protected files or keyrings, or hardware such as TPMs, smartcards, secure co-processors, and hardware security modules.

Key distribution asks how legitimate users securely receive keys; key storage asks how those keys remain protected after receipt. Secure cryptography depends on both, as well as replacement and destruction when keys are no longer valid.

Attack goals and cryptanalysis Slide 37

Attack goals include recovering a key, full or partial plaintext, a property of plaintext, producing valid ciphertext, causing a predictable plaintext change by modifying ciphertext, or forging an authentication tag or signature.

  • Ciphertext-only: the adversary has only ciphertext.
  • Known-plaintext: the adversary has plaintext and matching ciphertext.
  • Chosen-plaintext: the adversary chooses plaintext and obtains ciphertext.
  • Chosen-ciphertext: the adversary chooses ciphertext and learns plaintext or a decryption-related response.
  • Common approaches: brute force; linear cryptanalysis, which uses biased linear relations among plaintext, ciphertext, and key bits; differential cryptanalysis, which studies how input differences affect output differences; side channels involving timing, power, caches, or electromagnetic emissions; and coercion to obtain a key or password directly.

The more information or control an attacker has, the stronger the attack model and the more robust the cryptosystem must be. A system is secure only if it resists the attack goals that matter under its stated threat model.

Security concepts

One-time pad and perfect secrecy Slide 42

A one-time pad (OTP) uses a shared, secret, uniformly random key $k$ that is as long as the message $m$. It encrypts and decrypts with XOR:

c = m ⊕ k    m = c ⊕ k = (m ⊕ k) ⊕ k    (A ⊕ B) ⊕ B = A
  • The key must be uniformly random, at least as long as the message, used only once, and destroyed after use.
  • Under those assumptions, OTP provides perfect secrecy: ciphertext gives an attacker no information about which plaintext was sent, even with unlimited computation.
  • For any observed ciphertext, every candidate plaintext remains possible under some key. The ciphertext therefore does not change an adversary's knowledge of which plaintext was sent.
  • Perfect secrecy does not provide integrity. An attacker may be unable to read ciphertext but can still alter it.
  • OTP is operationally difficult because long or numerous messages require enormous amounts of securely distributed random key material.

Why OTP key reuse fails Slide 45

C1 = M1 ⊕ K    C2 = M2 ⊕ K
C1 ⊕ C2 = (M1 ⊕ K) ⊕ (M2 ⊕ K) = M1 ⊕ M2

When the same key encrypts two messages, XORing their ciphertexts cancels the key because $K \oplus K = 0$. The result exposes a relationship between the plaintexts; natural-language patterns, known text, and guesses can then reveal both messages. The Venona project is a historical example of OTP key reuse breaking a system: Soviet intelligence generated pads with typists, then reused pages during World War II, allowing the United States to decrypt about 2,200 messages and expose espionage in the United Kingdom and the Manhattan Project.

Information-theoretic and computational security

Information-theoretic security Computational security
Secure even against unlimited computation. A correctly used OTP is the standard example, but it often requires key material as large as the protected data. Also called conditional security. It relies on bounded attacker resources or on mathematical problems believed hard to reverse, such as the assumption that $P \ne NP$. DES, 3DES, AES, RSA, DSA, ECC, and Diffie-Hellman are examples of deployed systems or primitives using this model; the notes also list MD5 and SHA. Nearly all deployed cryptosystems use it.

Historical ciphers: educational, not secure

Cryptography developed from pen-and-paper methods, through electro-mechanical machines, to computer-based systems. These older ciphers build intuition about key spaces, brute force, frequency analysis, and pattern leakage, but they are not suitable for modern use.

  • Caesar cipher: Slide 53 shifts each letter by key $x$. $c = (p + x) \bmod 26$ and $p = (c - x) \bmod 26$; with $x = 3$, HELLO becomes KHOOR. It has only 26 shifts, so brute force and frequency analysis break it easily. The ROT13 exercise $GUVF\ VF\ N\ TERNG\ PYNFF$ decodes to "THIS IS A GREAT CLASS."
  • ROT13: Slide 55 a Caesar shift of 13. Applying it twice restores the message; HELLO becomes URYYB and URYYB becomes HELLO. Because the alphabet has 26 letters, the encryption and decryption operations are effectively the same. It was used on Usenet to obscure spoilers and sensitive text in the 1980s, not to provide security.
  • Monoalphabetic substitution: Slide 57 maps each letter through one fixed pattern. For example, a mapping could begin $A \rightarrow U$, $B \rightarrow X$, $C \rightarrow A$. Even with $26!$ possible substitutions, repeated letters, word boundaries, and language frequencies leak structure.
  • Homophonic substitution: allows one plaintext symbol to map to multiple ciphertext symbols, such as $a \rightarrow ZA$ or $b \rightarrow HLK$, which makes simple frequency analysis harder but does not make it modern secure encryption.
  • Polyalphabetic and Vigenere ciphers: change substitutions as a repeating key is applied. The repeating key is a weakness: Kasiski examination uses repeated ciphertext patterns and their distances to estimate key length.

Frequency and pattern analysis Slide 58

  • Common English letters such as $e$, $t$, and $a$; bigrams such as $th$, $he$, and $en$; and trigrams such as $the$, $and$, and $tha$ provide clues.
  • Repeated letters, word lengths, word boundaries, repeated words, and sentence context preserve useful structure in substitution ciphers.
  • A large key space alone does not make a cipher secure if it leaks recognizable language patterns.

Polyalphabetic ciphers and Kasiski examination Slide 61

A polyalphabetic cipher uses multiple monoalphabetic substitutions, so plaintext letters can map to different ciphertext letters and simple frequency distributions are masked. The Alberti cipher (1467), one of the first polyalphabetic ciphers, used a rotating cipher wheel based on the portable printing press to change the substitution every few letters. Its illustration shows a letter starting at $g \rightarrow A$, then moving at the next rotation from $g \rightarrow Q$.

Vigenere is a polyalphabetic substitution cipher that repeats a key across the plaintext. For plaintext cryptois shortforcryptography and repeating key ABCDABCDABCDABCDABCDABCDABCD, the document gives ciphertext CSASTPKVSIQUTGQUCSASTPIUAQJB. It was once called le chiffre indechiffrable, was first described in 1553, and was not broken for about 300 years, but its repeating key is a weakness.

Kasiski examination finds repeated ciphertext patterns and measures their spacing. In the example, CSASTP repeats 16 positions later, so $16$, $8$, $4$, $2$, and $1$ are possible key lengths. If the same plaintext pattern is encrypted under the same part of a repeating key, it can produce the same ciphertext pattern. Longer messages provide more repeated patterns and improve the estimate.

Classical ciphers are useful for studying key spaces, brute force, frequency analysis, and pattern leakage. They should not be used for modern cryptography.
