Answers

Explain definitions of security

Definition 1: Security prevents access by unauthorized users.

Definition 2: A computer is secure when you can depend on it and its software to behave as expected.

Course definition: A system is secure if it maintains well-specified properties despite the actions of well-specified adversaries. The participant, conditions, and time period must be stated.

Define adversary, trust, threat, and security models

Adversary: anybody attempting to circumvent security infrastructure. State what the adversary can access, observe, modify, or control.

Trust: the degree to which an entity is expected to behave in the system.

Threat model: a description of the threats, adversaries, capabilities, and assumptions relevant to a system.

Trust model: a description of who is trusted to do what in a particular environment.

Security model: the combination of a trust model and a threat model that addresses perceived risks.

Define security properties and system vocabulary

CIA Triad
  • Confidentiality: prevent unauthorized disclosure.
  • Integrity: prevent unauthorized modification.
  • Availability: preserve authorized access.
System vocabulary
  • Asset: something valuable that we protect.
  • Participant: an entity that interacts with the system, including people, computers, agents, servers, clients, and hosts.
  • Vulnerability: a flaw that exposes an asset or system to a threat.
  • Threat: a possible danger that may exploit a vulnerability.
  • Attack: an attempt to exploit a vulnerability.
  • Defense: a measure that reduces the potential or impact of an attack.
  • TCB: Trusted Computing Base, the components critical to system security.
  • Risk: expected loss, modeled as R = T x V x C or R = P x C.

Identify attacker, attack, and defense archetypes

Attacker types: curious or clueless users, script kiddies, people with an axe to grind, organized criminals, competitors, and governments or nation-states.

Attack archetypes: interception, modification, fabrication, and interruption.

Defense archetypes: prevention, deterrence, deflection, detection, and recovery.

Differentiate trusted and trustworthy

Trusted: a component is relied upon because its compromise could break the security policy.

Trustworthy: there is evidence or assurance that a component will behave correctly under stated conditions. A component can be trusted without being proven trustworthy.

Create and articulate a security model

Identify the assets and required properties; participants and trust assumptions; the TCB; adversaries and capabilities; vulnerabilities and threats; and attacks and defenses. State the model's assumptions and limits, then reassess it as the system and threats change.

Define adequate protection

Protect assets to a degree consistent with their value. More security is not automatically better because controls cost money, performance, resources, usability, and complexity. A control is easier to justify when its cost is lower than the expected loss it prevents.

Slides 1-6: Course context and objectives

Lecture 02 is the foundations lecture on security fundamentals. The stated objectives are to explain definitions of security; define adversary, trust, threat, and security models; define confidentiality, integrity, availability, asset, participant, vulnerability, threat, attack, defense, TCB, and risk; identify attacker, attack, and defense archetypes; differentiate trusted and trustworthy; create security models; and define adequate protection.

Slides 7-12: What is security?

The deck presents security as context-dependent. Harrison, Ruzzo, and Ullman describe preventing access by unauthorized users. Garfinkel and Spafford describe a computer as secure when you can depend on it and its software to behave as expected. The deck then asks: As expected by whom? Under what conditions? When? The course meta-definition is that a system is secure when it maintains well-specified properties despite well-specified adversaries.

Slides 13-16: ATM case study and core properties

The Glenwood Avenue ATM is used as a case study for constructing a security model. The system must protect money, PINs, account information, transaction records, balances, software, and authorized customer access. Withdrawals should be atomic: dispensing cash and updating the account must succeed consistently or not occur.

The CIA triad is confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Confidentiality protects against unauthorized disclosure; integrity protects against unauthorized modification; and availability protects authorized access to information or services. Which property matters most depends on the asset and context. The slides use stained stolen cash and emergency communications to show that integrity, availability, and confidentiality can be traded off.

Slides 17-25: System vocabulary

An asset is something we protect, including systems, access, people, attention, data, money, and reputation. A participant is a system entity, not only a person: computers, agents, enterprises, servers, clients, hosts, and users can participate. Security can look different to different participants; a bank and a customer may protect different assets and expect different outcomes.

An adversary attempts to circumvent security infrastructure. Examples include curious users or script kiddies, people with an axe to grind, organized criminals, competitors, and governments or nation-states. Users may be treated as adversaries in some models, but the slides also present the human-centered view that users are not the enemy.

Slides 26-37: Vulnerability, threat, and risk

A vulnerability, or attack vector, is a flaw that exposes a user, data, or system to a threat. A threat is a potential danger that may exploit a vulnerability. Threats can be intentional, accidental, or environmental. A broken server-room window is a vulnerability; an intruder is a possible threat that could exploit it.

R = T x V x C   and, with P as combined success probability,   R = P x C

The deck's ATM example estimates a 30% monthly chance of a stolen-card attempt, a 60% chance of successful exploitation, and an $800 cost: 0.3 x 0.6 x $800 = $144 expected loss per month. Reducing threat likelihood or vulnerability to 20% gives $48; reducing cost to $100 gives $18.

Slides 38-46: Attacks and defenses

An attack attempts to exploit a vulnerability; a compromise is a successful attack. The four attack archetypes are interception, modification, fabrication, and interruption. They can overlap: ransomware is modification plus interruption because it encrypts files and blocks access.

The five defense archetypes are prevention, deterrence, deflection, detection, and recovery. Prevention blocks an attack or closes a vulnerability; deterrence makes an attack harder or less attractive; deflection makes a target less desirable; detection identifies an attack in progress; and recovery restores service or data.

Slides 47-53: Threat modeling

A threat model is a systematic identification of the threats a system faces. One approach is to brainstorm known threats, rank them by risk using likelihood and impact, and choose responses, techniques, and implementations. Threat modeling is iterative: scope the system, map data flows and trust boundaries, find threats using multiple approaches, mitigate them, and reassess the model as threats and attacker capabilities change.

STRIDE asks about spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, and elevation of privilege. In academic papers, a threat model commonly states adversaries, goals and motivations, capabilities, the TCB, and other assumptions.

Slides 54-60: Authentication, authorization, and trust

Authentication asks, “Who are you?” Authorization asks, “What are you allowed to do?” Authentication alone is not enough because identifying an entity does not determine which actions or resources it may access. A trust model describes who is trusted to do what in a particular environment.

The trusted computing base, or TCB, is the set of components critical to system security. A trusted component is one whose compromise would break the security policy. A trustworthy component is one for which there is reason or a guarantee to expect correct behavior under stated conditions. An ATM operating system is trusted because the ATM depends on it, but it is not automatically trustworthy.

Slides 61-64: Security model

A security model is the combination of a trust model and a threat model that addresses perceived risks. It asks: What are the security concerns and threats? Who are our adversaries? Who do we trust and to do what? The result becomes the security requirements used to develop a coherent design. Every secure design needs a security model, and it is difficult to retrofit security after deployment.

Slides 65-69: Security economics

Security costs money, resources, performance, and complexity. More security is not automatically better because controls add cost and failure points. The principle of adequate protection says computer items must be protected only until they lose their value and to a degree consistent with their value. A defense is easier to justify when its cost is lower than the expected loss it reduces, and the appropriate protection can change over time.

For the ATM, the deck asks which protections are worth paying for: moving it indoors behind locked doors but losing 24/7 street access, adding cameras, lighting, and an alarm, adding anti-skimmer checks and PIN shielding, adding a guard overnight, or reducing the cash it holds.

1. What does secure mean?

There is no universal definition of security. Security depends on the participant, the asset, the properties being protected, the adversary, and the conditions of the claim.

Course definitionA system is secure if it can maintain well-specified properties despite the actions of well-specified adversaries.

The slide definitions raise three questions: As expected by whom? Under what conditions? When?

Harrison, Ruzzo, and Ullman describe security as preventing access by unauthorized users. Garfinkel and Spafford describe a computer as secure when you can depend on it and its software to behave as expected. These definitions are useful, but they still require context: expected by whom, under what conditions, and when?

2. The CIA triad

ConfidentialityPrevent unauthorized disclosure of information.
IntegrityPrevent unauthorized modification of data, code, or configuration.
AvailabilityPreserve authorized access to information and systems.

Which CIA property matters most depends on the asset, context, and consequences. The properties can conflict.

Confidentiality protects against unauthorized disclosure, including content, metadata, relationships, and sometimes even the existence of communication. Integrity protects data, configuration, code, and behavior from unauthorized modification. Availability protects authorized access to an information system. An ATM may prioritize integrity by staining stolen cash, while emergency responders may prioritize availability by using an unencrypted fallback channel.

3. Applying a security model to an ATM

Return to the Glenwood Avenue ATM example. Important properties include confidentiality of PINs, account information, and transaction records; integrity of balances, withdrawal amounts, software, and cash dispensing; availability of cash, network connectivity, and authorized customer access; and atomic withdrawals.

Possible adversaries include physical attackers, skimmer operators, shoulder surfers, hackers, criminals, users, and environmental events. Mechanisms include hardened cases, locks, PIN shielding, anti-skimmer checks, transaction verification, cash sensors, backup power, and redundant network connections.

A secure ATM must also make withdrawals atomic: either the cash is dispensed and the account is updated consistently, or neither occurs. Other possible mechanisms include withdrawal limits, software checks, gas detection, and venting systems.

4. Security vocabulary

Assets are what we protect. Participants are system entities, including people, computers, agents, servers, clients, and hosts. An adversary attempts to circumvent security. A vulnerability is a flaw that exposes an asset to a threat. A threat is a possible danger that may exploit a vulnerability.

R = T x V x C   or, simplified,   R = P x C

Here, T is threat likelihood, V is the probability of successful exploitation, P is the combined probability of success, and C is cost or impact. The ATM example uses 0.30 x 0.60 x $800 = $144 expected loss per month.

A broken window in a server room is best classified as a vulnerability: it is a flaw or condition that exposes the system. An intruder is a possible threat that could exploit it. Threats may be intentional, accidental, or environmental.

5. Attack and defense archetypes

Attacks include interception (unauthorized access), modification (unauthorized change), fabrication (introducing something false), and interruption (making an asset unavailable or unusable). Ransomware is modification plus interruption.

Defenses include prevention, deterrence, deflection, detection, and recovery. Attacks and defenses can involve multiple types.

For example, packet sniffing is interception, changing a bank-transfer amount is modification, forging a password-reset message is fabrication, and denial of service is interruption. Ransomware that encrypts files is both modification and interruption.

6. Threat modeling

A threat model is a systematic identification of the threats a system faces. Brainstorm known threats, rank them by likelihood and impact, choose responses, and reassess the model as the system and attacker capabilities change.

STRIDE: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, and elevation of privilege.

For an academic paper, ask: Who are potential adversaries? What are their goals and motivations? What are their capabilities? What is the TCB? What other assumptions does the paper make?

Threat modeling is iterative. Scope the system, map data flows and trust boundaries, identify threats with approaches such as STRIDE, rank them by likelihood and impact, choose mitigations, and reassess as the system and adversary capabilities change. If the threat model is wrong, the system remains vulnerable to the threat that was missed.

7. Authentication, authorization, and trust

Authentication answers “Who are you?” Authorization answers “What are you allowed to do?” A student and an instructor may authenticate successfully but receive different authorization.

A trust model describes who is trusted to do what. The trusted computing base (TCB) is the set of hardware and software components critical to system security, such as firmware, a boot chain, a kernel, a reference monitor, or an ATM's internal control system.

A trusted component is one whose compromise would break a security policy. A trustworthy component is one we have reason to expect will behave correctly under stated conditions. These terms are related but not interchangeable.

For the ATM, the operating system is trusted because the ATM depends on it to perform security- critical work. That does not automatically prove that the operating system is trustworthy. Trust decisions are made even when systems are not completely secure.

8. The security model

A security model combines the trust model and the threat model. It identifies assets, properties, participants, assumptions, adversaries, capabilities, threats, and defenses.

Design principleBuild the security model before deployment. Security is difficult to retrofit after a system's architecture and interfaces are already fixed.

The model must evolve as new vulnerabilities, technologies, and attacker capabilities appear.

Every secure design needs a security model. A lack of a coherent model is a major security mistake, and security is difficult to retrofit after architecture and interfaces are fixed.

9. Security economics

Security costs money, performance, resources, and complexity. Controls can introduce additional failure points, so more security is not automatically better.

Principle of adequate protectionProtect assets to a degree consistent with their value. A defense is easier to justify when its cost is lower than the expected loss it prevents.

For an ATM, a camera, lighting, alarms, keypad shielding, cash limits, and tamper checks may be reasonable. Moving the machine behind locked doors or assigning an armed guard may cost more than the risk justifies. The right choice changes with the asset's value, the environment, and the adversaries.

The principle of adequate protection says computer items should be protected only until they lose their value and to a degree consistent with that value. Security also adds complexity and failure points, so a control is easiest to justify when its cost is lower than the expected loss it reduces.

Questions asked during lecture

These prompts are preserved from the professor's discussion and placed with their lecture-based answers for practice.

How would you define when a system is secure?

There is no universal definition. For this course, a system is secure if it can maintain well-specified properties despite the actions of well-specified adversaries. The properties, adversaries, capabilities, and conditions must be stated because security is context-dependent.

What properties do you think it needs to maintain or should maintain at least?

For the ATM, protect PINs, account information, transaction records, cash, and correct balances; dispense the correct amount; prevent unauthorized access; and keep withdrawals atomic so a failure cannot dispense money without updating the account.

What type of adversaries do you think you have or like this ATM?

Possible adversaries include physical attackers, skimmer operators, shoulder surfers, hackers, organized criminals, users who make mistakes, and environmental events such as fire, weather, power loss, or other natural causes.

What mechanisms do you think this ATM has to prevent some of these things?

Examples include a hardened case and locks, shielded or recessed keypads, tamper-evident seals, cash sensors, withdrawal limits, transaction verification, backup power, redundant network connections, gas detection, and venting systems.

Which three of those do you think matters most for security?

The answer depends on the system and context. Confidentiality, integrity, and availability can conflict: an ATM may sacrifice availability to protect the integrity of stolen cash, while emergency responders may sacrifice confidentiality to preserve an available communication channel.

Would you say users are adversaries?

It depends on the context. Users may be treated as adversaries when they circumvent controls or violate policies, but the human-centered view is that users are not the enemy. Security should account for their context, information, and task constraints instead of forcing unsafe workarounds.

What type of attack would you say is packet sniffing on a public Wi-Fi?

Interception: the attacker gains unauthorized access to network traffic.

What would you say of these four is changing a bank transfer amount?

Modification: the attacker makes an unauthorized change to transaction data.

What is forging a password reset email so we can trick the user into maybe entering their password on a phishing website?

Fabrication creates a false artifact; the theft of credentials can also involve interception.

What would you say is a denial of service against the login service?

Interruption: the attack disrupts access to or use of the login service.

What would you say is a ransomware that encrypts files?

Modification plus interruption: ransomware changes the files by encrypting them and prevents normal access to them. Attack archetypes can overlap.

What would you say of these five defenses is having backups of your systems?

Recovery: backups help restore data or service after an attack or failure.

What is the difference between authentication and authorization?

Authentication asks, “Who are you?” Authorization asks, “What are you allowed to do?” A participant can authenticate successfully but have limited authorization.

How can we come up with a security model?

Identify assets and properties; participants and trust assumptions; adversaries and capabilities; vulnerabilities and threats; and defenses. State the model's limits, then reassess it as systems, threats, and attacker capabilities change.

How important is it to you that your home is safe?

Locks, alarms, monitoring, and armed guards provide different levels of protection at different costs. More security is not automatically the economic choice.

Would you invest in moving this ATM behind locked doors but lose 24-hour street access?

Evaluate the cost and lost convenience against the expected reduction in risk under the principle of adequate protection.
