ATM Model: Security Fundamentals in One System

The Glenwood Avenue ATM is the lecture's running case study. A security model starts by naming the properties the ATM must maintain and the adversaries that may act against it, then selects mechanisms that maintain those properties despite those adversaries.

Security modelTrust model + Threat model = Security modelFor this ATM, state the assets and required properties, participants and trust assumptions, adversaries and capabilities, credible threats and vulnerabilities, and the controls that address the resulting risk.

Assets, participants, and required properties

Model element ATM application
Assets Cash, PINs, card and account data, transaction records, balances, ATM software, network access, customer time, and the bank's reputation.
Participants Customers, the bank and its backend server, the ATM provider and technicians, the payment network, and the ATM itself. The bank and customer can view the same ATM differently because their assets and expectations differ.
Confidentiality Prevent unauthorized disclosure of PINs, card and account information, and transaction records.
Integrity Prevent unauthorized changes to balances, withdrawal amounts, software, configuration, transaction records, and cash dispensing.
Availability Preserve authorized customer access to cash, the ATM, and the network service.
Atomicity A withdrawal must dispense cash and update the account consistently, or do neither. The ATM must not dispense $100 without recording the $100 withdrawal.

Trust model and TCB

Customers trust the bank not to leak their data and to return correct account information. The bank trusts the ATM, its network connection, and its operational processes to carry out authorized transactions. These are assumptions, not guarantees.

The ATM operating system, firmware and boot chain, hardware protections, transaction software, cryptographic keys, and bank authorization service are security-critical components of the Trusted Computing Base (TCB). The operating system is trusted because the ATM depends on it; it is not automatically trustworthy, which requires reason to expect correct behavior under stated conditions.

Threat model: adversaries, capabilities, and weaknesses

Adversary Goal or capability Vulnerability or threat
Ordinary thief or physical attacker Open, remove, or damage the ATM; steal cash; interrupt service. Weak enclosure, poor placement, or inadequate physical monitoring.
Skimmer operator or card thief Capture card data and PINs to make fraudulent withdrawals. Skimming hardware, exposed keypad entry, or stolen credentials.
Technically skilled attacker Manipulate ATM software, the network, or transaction messages. Unpatched software, insecure configuration, or weak network authentication.
Fraudster with stolen credentials Authenticate as a customer and withdraw cash. Compromised cards or PINs, excessive transaction limits, or missed fraud signals.
Malicious insider Misuse legitimate maintenance or bank access. Overly broad authorization, weak auditing, or insufficient separation of duties.
Environmental or accidental event Cause a power, network, or hardware outage. Single points of failure and missing backup or recovery procedures.

Attacks and defenses

A stolen-card withdrawal can involve interception of credentials, fabrication of fraudulent transaction data, modification of a balance or transaction, and interruption when the service or cash is made unavailable. One incident may use more than one attack archetype.

Defense archetype ATM mechanisms
Prevention Card-and-PIN authentication, encryption, access controls, secure configuration, software updates, transaction limits, and network authentication.
Deterrence Visible cameras, lighting, warning signs, tamper-resistant enclosure, and card lockout after repeated PIN failures.
Deflection Make this machine a less appealing target through anti-skimming hardware, inspections, and protections that raise the effort needed for successful fraud.
Detection Transaction logs, audit records, tamper alerts, cameras, monitoring, and fraud detection.
Recovery Backup power and networking, incident response, restoring secure configurations, card replacement, and procedures for resolving disputed transactions.

Risk and adequate protection

R = T x V x C   or, with combined success probability,   R = P x C

For an unauthorized withdrawal using a stolen or fake card:

  • Threat likelihood ($T$) = 0.3: an estimated 30% monthly chance that someone attempts a fraudulent withdrawal.
  • Vulnerability ($V$) = 0.6: if an attack is attempted, an estimated 60% chance that the attacker has sufficient card or PIN information to succeed.
  • Consequence ($C$) = $800: estimated direct financial loss and response cost from one successful fraudulent withdrawal.
  • Baseline risk: $R = 0.3 x 0.6 x $800 = $144/month$.

Risk-reduction scenarios

  • Reduce threat with fraud monitoring and blocked-card lists:
    • $T = 0.3 \rightarrow 0.1$
    • New risk: $R = 0.1 x 0.6 x $800 = $48/month$.
    • Risk reduction: $144 - $48 = $96/month$.
  • Reduce vulnerability with a security audit and current industry controls:
    • $V = 0.6 \rightarrow 0.2$
    • New risk: $R = 0.3 x 0.2 x $800 = $48/month$.
    • Risk reduction: $144 - $48 = $96/month$.
  • Reduce consequence or transfer risk with insurance:
    • $C = $800 \rightarrow $100$
    • New risk: $R = 0.3 x 0.6 x $100 = $18/month$.
    • Risk reduction: $144 - $18 = $126/month$.

By these estimates alone, insurance produces the largest expected financial reduction: $144/month \rightarrow $18/month. It does not make fraud less likely or the ATM less vulnerable; it transfers much of the loss. The control is economically justified only when its cost is reasonable compared with the expected reduction in loss.

The principle of adequate protection asks whether a defense costs less than the expected loss it reduces. Camera coverage, lighting, alarms, anti-skimmer checks, PIN shielding, and lower cash limits can be cost-effective. Moving an ATM indoors can sacrifice 24/7 availability, and an overnight guard may cost more than the risk reduction justifies. The appropriate choice changes with the ATM's location, assets, adversaries, and operating conditions.

1. What does secure mean?

There is no universal definition of security. Security depends on the participant, the asset, the properties being protected, the adversary, and the conditions of the claim.

Course definitionA system is secure if it can maintain well-specified properties despite the actions of well-specified adversaries.

The slide definitions raise three questions: As expected by whom? Under what conditions? When?

Harrison, Ruzzo, and Ullman describe security as preventing access by unauthorized users. Garfinkel and Spafford describe a computer as secure when you can depend on it and its software to behave as expected. These definitions are useful, but they still require context: expected by whom, under what conditions, and when?

From a definition to a security model

  • The properties assumed to hold form the trust model.
  • The adversaries and their capabilities form the threat model.
  • Trust model + Threat model = Security model.

To create a secure design, first name the properties that must be maintained and the adversaries that may act against the system. Then build mechanisms that maintain those properties despite those adversaries.

What a complete security claim must state

  • Properties: what the system must preserve, such as confidentiality, integrity, availability, or another system-specific property.
  • Assumptions and conditions: the environment, operating conditions, and components expected to behave as assumed.
  • Adversaries: who might attempt to violate the properties and what they can access, observe, modify, or control.
  • Participant viewpoint and time: whose expectation is being evaluated and when the claim is intended to hold.

2. The CIA triad

ConfidentialityPrevent unauthorized disclosure of information.
IntegrityPrevent unauthorized modification of data, code, or configuration.
AvailabilityPreserve authorized access to information and systems.

Which CIA property matters most depends on the asset, context, and consequences. The properties can conflict.

Confidentiality protects against unauthorized disclosure, including content, metadata, relationships, and sometimes even the existence of communication. Integrity protects data, configuration, code, and behavior from unauthorized modification. Availability protects authorized access to an information system. An ATM may prioritize integrity by staining stolen cash, while emergency responders may prioritize availability by using an unencrypted fallback channel.

Context determines the priority

  • ATM: ink staining can make stolen cash unavailable, but discourages theft; integrity can take priority over availability.
  • Online banking or stock trading: a service can halt transfers or trading when transaction data appears suspicious; integrity can take priority over availability.
  • Hospital records or aircraft control: extra verification or rejecting a questionable command can delay access, but altered information or commands can cause greater harm; integrity can take priority over availability.
  • Emergency response and 911 systems: a less-secure fallback channel can be preferable to no communication; availability can take priority over confidentiality.
  • Military plans, classified documents, and password databases: denying access can be preferable to exposing sensitive information; confidentiality can take priority over availability.
  • Public websites and streaming services: published content has little confidentiality need compared with reliable access; availability can take priority over confidentiality.
  • Electronic voting: temporarily stopping a system can be preferable to recording altered votes; integrity can take priority over availability.
  • Cloud storage and backup: delaying restoration until backups are verified can be preferable to restoring corrupted or ransomware-encrypted files; integrity can take priority over availability.

Examples of each property

  • Confidentiality: prevent Alice from reading Bob's files without permission; prevent Bob from learning that Alice has a sensitive file; prevent an eavesdropper from reading network traffic; or prevent someone from learning that Alice is a clinic patient.
  • Integrity: prevent unauthorized changes to files, network traffic, transaction data, code, or system configuration; prevent replacement of an important system binary.
  • Availability: prevent a site or service from crashing, being flooded with requests, or otherwise becoming unusable by authorized users.
Study shortcut: prioritize confidentiality when a secret leaking is the greatest harm, integrity when incorrect or altered information is the greatest harm, and availability when losing access altogether is the greatest harm.

4. Security vocabulary

Assets are what we protect. Participants are system entities, including people, computers, agents, servers, clients, and hosts. An adversary attempts to circumvent security. A vulnerability is a flaw that exposes an asset to a threat. A threat is a possible danger that may exploit a vulnerability.

R = T x V x C   or, simplified,   R = P x C

Here, T is threat likelihood, V is the probability of successful exploitation, P is the combined probability of success, and C is cost or impact. The ATM example uses 0.30 x 0.60 x $800 = $144 expected loss per month.

A broken window in a server room is best classified as a vulnerability: it is a flaw or condition that exposes the system. An intruder is a possible threat that could exploit it. Threats may be intentional, accidental, or environmental.

Terms in context

  • Assets can be systems (hardware, operating systems, and software), access (networks, accounts, and privileges), people (including user time and attention), and value such as data, money, and reputation.
  • Participants can be principals, servers, clients, users, entities, hosts, routers, computers, agents, people, or enterprises. A system can appear secure to one participant and insecure to another because their assets, expectations, and capabilities differ.
  • Trusted third party: an entity trusted by all relevant parties for a defined set of actions. For example, customers trust a bank not to leak data and to return a correct account balance.
  • Adversaries include curious or generally clueless users, script kiddies, people with an axe to grind, organized criminals, competitors, and nation-state actors. Authorized users can also violate policy deliberately or carelessly; usable-security design considers their context, available information, and task constraints.
  • Vulnerabilities include buffer overflows and WEP key leakage. They can result from faulty software or hardware, poor requirements or design, insecure policy or configuration, system misuse, or an unintended purpose or environment.
  • Threats can be intentional, such as an attacker, or accidental and environmental, such as an earthquake, fire, tornado, or power failure. A successful attack needs an applicable weakness to exploit.

5. Attack and defense archetypes

Attacks include interception (unauthorized access), modification (unauthorized change), fabrication (introducing something false), and interruption (making an asset unavailable or unusable). Ransomware is modification plus interruption.

Defenses include prevention, deterrence, deflection, detection, and recovery. Attacks and defenses can involve multiple types.

For example, packet sniffing is interception, changing a bank-transfer amount is modification, forging a password-reset message is fabrication, and denial of service is interruption. Ransomware that encrypts files is both modification and interruption.

Attack archetypes can overlap

  • Phishing login page: fabrication creates the fake site; interception occurs when the attacker collects credentials.
  • Transaction tampering: interception occurs when an attacker observes a payment request; modification occurs when they alter its amount or destination.
  • Ransomware: modification encrypts or changes files; interruption blocks normal access to services that rely on those files.

Defense archetypes in practice

  • Prevention: block an attack or close the vulnerability. Rate limiting can block high-volume attempts.
  • Deterrence: make an attack harder or less attractive, without making it impossible. Warning banners can increase an attacker's perceived risk; rate limiting can also deter costly repeated attempts.
  • Deflection: make this target less desirable than alternatives. A hidden service can be harder to discover or less attractive to attack.
  • Detection: identify an attack in progress and respond. Logging records evidence so attacks can be noticed and investigated.
  • Recovery: assume an attack may occur and prepare to restore data or service. Backups support recovery after a failure or compromise.

6. Threat modeling

A threat model is a systematic identification of the threats a system faces. Brainstorm known threats, rank them by likelihood and impact, choose responses, and reassess the model as the system and attacker capabilities change.

STRIDE: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, and elevation of privilege.
STRIDE threat Desired security property
Spoofing Authenticity
Tampering Integrity
Repudiation Non-repudiability
Information disclosure Confidentiality
Denial of service Availability
Elevation of privilege Authorization

For an academic paper, ask: Who are potential adversaries? What are their goals and motivations? What are their capabilities? What is the TCB? What other assumptions does the paper make?

Threat modeling is iterative. Scope the system, map data flows and trust boundaries, identify threats with approaches such as STRIDE, rank them by likelihood and impact, choose mitigations, and reassess as the system and adversary capabilities change. If the threat model is wrong, the system remains vulnerable to the threat that was missed.

Iterative threat-model workflow

  • Scope: identify what the system does, where data flows, and where trust changes.
  • Find threats: combine diagrams, STRIDE, misuse cases, and known threat lists.
  • Mitigate: map threats to controls, design changes, or explicit risk acceptance.
  • Assess: review whether the model covers the system and supports sound decisions.

Useful artifacts include data-flow diagrams, which reveal trust boundaries and hidden assumptions; use and misuse cases, which compare expected and attacker workflows; and risk ranking, which compares impact, likelihood, and ease of exploitation on a consistent scale.

Other useful lenses include an asset inventory, entry points, trust boundaries, attack trees, STRIDE checklists, misuse cases, LINDDUN, and risk ranking. Different lenses expose different assumptions and attack paths.

7. Authentication, authorization, and trust

Authentication answers “Who are you?” Authorization answers “What are you allowed to do?” A student and an instructor may authenticate successfully but receive different authorization.

A trust model describes who is trusted to do what. The trusted computing base (TCB) is the set of hardware and software components critical to system security, such as firmware, a boot chain, a kernel, a reference monitor, or an ATM's internal control system.

A trusted component is one whose compromise would break a security policy. A trustworthy component is one we have reason to expect will behave correctly under stated conditions. These terms are related but not interchangeable.

For the ATM, the operating system is trusted because the ATM depends on it to perform security- critical work. That does not automatically prove that the operating system is trustworthy. Trust decisions are made even when systems are not completely secure.

Trust decisions and TCB boundaries

  • Everyday trust decisions: trusting a phone or computer with data, a website with a password or payment, a Wi-Fi network with traffic, or an app with location, camera, or contacts permissions.
  • Inside a typical TCB: CPU and memory isolation, firmware and the boot chain, the kernel and reference monitor, and security-critical services.
  • Outside a typical TCB: ordinary applications, user workflows, and network peers. Their compromise can still be harmful, but the core security policy is not assumed to depend on their correct operation in the same way.
  • Trusted examples: a kernel, root certificate authority, or payment processor can be trusted because its failure can break the security policy. Audited code or a hardened service provides evidence of trustworthiness.

8. The security model

A security model combines the trust model and the threat model. It identifies assets, properties, participants, assumptions, adversaries, capabilities, threats, and defenses.

Design principleBuild the security model before deployment. Security is difficult to retrofit after a system's architecture and interfaces are already fixed.

The model must evolve as new vulnerabilities, technologies, and attacker capabilities appear.

Every secure design needs a security model. A lack of a coherent model is a major security mistake, and security is difficult to retrofit after architecture and interfaces are fixed.

9. Security economics

Security costs money, performance, resources, and complexity. Controls can introduce additional failure points, so more security is not automatically better.

Principle of adequate protectionProtect assets to a degree consistent with their value. A defense is easier to justify when its cost is lower than the expected loss it prevents.

For an ATM, a camera, lighting, alarms, keypad shielding, cash limits, and tamper checks may be reasonable. Moving the machine behind locked doors or assigning an armed guard may cost more than the risk justifies. The right choice changes with the asset's value, the environment, and the adversaries.

The principle of adequate protection says computer items should be protected only until they lose their value and to a degree consistent with that value. Security also adds complexity and failure points, so a control is easiest to justify when its cost is lower than the expected loss it reduces.

What security costs

  • Performance and usability.
  • Developer, administrator, educator, and user time.
  • Capital for hardware, replacement devices, or more servers.
  • Services such as denial-of-service protection, antivirus subscriptions, and monitoring.
  • Additional complexity and potential failure points.

The home-security comparison makes the same point: locks may be justified, while monitored alarms or overnight guards depend on the asset value and expected benefit. More protection is not automatically the economic choice. Unlikely attacks may not merit a defense when its deployment cost exceeds the expected compromise cost.

Would you say users are adversaries?

It depends on the context. Users may be treated as adversaries when they circumvent controls or violate policies, but the human-centered view is that users are not the enemy. Security should account for their context, information, and task constraints instead of forcing unsafe workarounds.

What is the difference between authentication and authorization?

Authentication asks, “Who are you?” Authorization asks, “What are you allowed to do?” A participant can authenticate successfully but have limited authorization.
